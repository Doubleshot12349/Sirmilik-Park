An Arctic Oasis

Birding Potential

First recognized by Europeans for it's importance to migratory birds, (Parks Canada Agency, 2025) Sirmilik National Park has more than 70 different species of birds that migrate or live on Bylot Island. The lush arctic wetland is an important stopping point for Greater Snow Geese, with over 70,000 birds spending their summer on the island. (Cadieux et.al.,2006) The Greater Snow Geese population drastically rose With the increase supply of food on their migratory route between Sirmilik National Park, the St. Lawrence river valley and the mid-atlantic United States thanks to large-scale agriculture. This created a need to manage the Snow Geese population to prevent them from disrupting the otherwise fragile artic ecosystem. Efforts are taken by the Bylot Island Monitoring Project and the Canadian Wildlife Agency to manage the area outside of the breeding season to limit the Goose population. (Cadieux et.al.,2006)

The Foxes and The Lemmings

Arctic Foxes are the primary predators on Bylot Island, along with Red Foxes and Snowy Owls. While primarily terrestial, they do range far distances across sea ice to scavenge from marine mammals that polar bears leave behind. This has resulted in a decline in their population as polar ice caps melt. (eco study) They have a White-Blue coat in the winter, but adopt a brown coat in the spring and summer. At 9 months old they mate for life and raise their pups in dens dug out of sandy soil or snow. Their population fluctuates in response to the cyclic pattern of their primary food source, lemmings, whose population keeps increasing until the plant life can't support them and they drop off. They don't always clearly follow this trend, as Arctic Foxes also eat berries, scavenge from caribou and marine mammal corpses, as well as hunt Greater Snow Geese goslings and eggs. Though this is done rarely, as they avoid fighting with angry geese parents. (ADFG,n.d.)

To Tusk or Not to Tusk

Narwhals live among the waters surrounding Sirmilik National Park, and are being threatened by hunting and melting sea ice. (Parks Canada Agency, 2025) They are medium-sized whales with a coat ranging from blue-gray to white as they age. (WWF,2026) This coloration inspired their name meaning corpse whale from norse, since they resemble drowned sailors. The feature they are most known for is their horn, really a long tooth coming from their forhead which European whalers took back home and told mythical stories about where it came from, giving rise to the unicorn myth. Unlike other whales, they remain in the arctic year round, feeding on halibut, cod, and squid beneath the ice. (NOAA,2020)
